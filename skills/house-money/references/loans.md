# Loans, rate locks, credits and insurance

## Choosing a lender

- Two kinds: direct lenders (banks and mortgage companies that fund and often service the loan) and mortgage brokers (who shop several lenders). Either can win; compare offers, not categories.
- Get official Loan Estimates from at least three lenders on the same day, for the same loan amount and lock period. Compare APR and total cost over the time the buyer expects to keep the loan, not the size of any "credit".
- Lender credits usually come from a slightly higher rate. That can be fine for a short hold and expensive over 30 years. Show both.
- Employer or affinity programs ("save up to $X") are one more Loan Estimate to compare. Ask whether the benefit is tied to a specific product or a higher rate, and what it is for this loan size. "Up to" is a ceiling, not a promise.
- Get a pre-approval that covers the offer price and is current.

## Rate locks

- Locks commonly run 30 to 60 days. Locking before the contract is signed usually wastes lock time; lock when the closing date is firm, with a buffer.
- Avoid locking in the hours right after a major data release while markets are moving, if the buyer has the choice.
- Know the extension cost before choosing a short lock.

## During underwriting

No new credit, no job change, no large unexplained deposits or transfers, no big purchases on credit (including furniture deposits). Ask the lender how gift funds must be documented (gift letter, source statements, timing) before moving them.

## Seller credits vs price reductions

- At the same seller net, a credit and a price cut change the monthly payment very little.
- Credits are capped by loan program rules (a percentage of price that depends on loan type and down payment) and cannot exceed the buyer's actual closing costs. Confirm the cap with the lender; excess credit is lost or must become a price cut.
- A credit keeps the price high, so the appraisal must support it. A price cut also lowers price-based taxes and title insurance.
- A mix (a price the seller can show, plus a credit up to the cap) is a common way to close a gap.

## Insurance before signing

Get a homeowner's insurance quote before signing the contract, and tell the insurer about known issues. Lenders require insurance, and most contracts have no insurance contingency. Common causes of refusal or high premiums: certain electrical panel brands, old roofs, buried oil tanks, flood zones, knob-and-tube wiring, open building violations. A written refusal is also the strongest negotiating document a buyer can have.

## Down payment promised in the offer

A down payment percentage written in the offer tells the seller the loan is safe. In attorney-driven markets it can often be adjusted before the contract is signed, when the attorney writes the loan amount into the mortgage contingency. After signing, changing it is a formal amendment. Verify local practice.

## Borrowing against assets abroad

Banks in most countries will not lend against US property. Borrowing against home-country assets and converting the cash swaps US rate risk for exchange-rate risk, and may bring capital-control paperwork. It is not free money; model it as currency exposure.
