# Stage map: from limits to move-in

Based on New York City practice and generalized. Where a step is known to differ by state, it says so. Anything marked "verify locally" is a pattern, not a rule for the buyer's location.

## 1. Stages at a glance

| # | Stage | Main output | Typical length | Who leads |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Limits | Cash available, monthly ceiling (conservative and real), reserve, must-haves | Before searching | Buyer |
| 1 | Shortlist | Public-record scan and listing forensics for each candidate | Days per house | Buyer, AI |
| 2 | Visit | Notes, measurements, questions for the seller | 1 to 2 visits per house | Buyer |
| 3 | Offer | Price, terms, walk-away number | Days | Buyer, buyer's agent |
| 4 | Inspection | Report, specialty tests, one consolidated request to the seller | 1 to 3 weeks | Inspector, buyer |
| 5 | Contract | Signed contract, deposit in escrow | 3 to 14 days, varies by state | Attorneys or agents |
| 6 | Mortgage to closing | Loan commitment, appraisal, title, insurance, final walkthrough | 30 to 60 days | Lender, attorney or title/escrow |
| 7 | After closing | Locks, utilities, permits, renovation order and budget | Weeks to months | Buyer, licensed trades |

## 2. Stage notes

**0. Limits.** Work out cash to close (down payment plus closing costs, not down payment alone), a monthly ceiling, and a cash reserve that stays untouched. Keep two ceilings side by side if the buyer has both a conservative rule and a real maximum; never quietly replace one with the other. Decide deal-breakers before falling for a house.

**1. Shortlist.** For each candidate: public records (violations, permits, sales and mortgage history, flood zone), listing forensics (floor plan and photos often answer the costliest question for free), commute tested at the real hour, and a check that the address is inside the jurisdiction you think it is (border towns differ in tax, schools and records).

**2. Visit.** Two visits at different times if the house is a real candidate. One person looks at systems (panel, heating, water heater, basement, signs of old oil tanks), the other at space (layout, light, storage, circulation). Record, do not judge, in front of the agents. Measure what decides legal use (see house-visit).

**3. Offer.** Price comes from sold comparables in the same ZIP code and condition band, time on market, and the seller's situation. Terms matter as much as price: deposit size, down payment percentage (a signal of loan strength), closing date, contingencies. The walk-away number comes from the money model, not from how much the buyer likes the house.

**4. Inspection.** General inspection plus the specialty tests the house's age and systems call for (sewer line camera, oil tank sweep, chimney, pests, radon, lead, asbestos). Ask for everything once, sorted by tier (see house-inspection). An insurance quote belongs in this stage: a refusal to insure is the strongest document a buyer can hold.

**5. Contract.** Know which document is which (attorney engagement letter, contract of sale, riders or addenda, disclosures). Check that the seller named in the contract is the owner of record. Check what the contract waives, what survives closing, and every deadline.

**6. Mortgage to closing.** Collect Loan Estimates from several lenders on the same day and compare total cost, not headline credits. Lock the rate when the closing date is firm. Put the loan commitment deadline in a calendar with the cancellation window. Get the insurance binder early. Verify wiring instructions by phone using a number you found yourself.

**7. After closing.** Change locks, find the main water, gas and electric shutoffs, move utilities, file for any tax exemptions. Before pulling permits, settle whether any existing unpermitted work needs to be legalized first, because an inspector on site for one permit can notice another problem.

## 3. Deadlines that cost money when missed

| Deadline | Where it comes from | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Inspection or option period end | Standard forms in many states (verify locally) | The buyer loses the right to walk away over condition |
| Attorney review period | Some states (New Jersey has one; verify locally) | The contract becomes binding as written |
| Loan commitment date and the cancellation window after it | Mortgage contingency clause | Missing the written notice can forfeit the deposit |
| Lead paint inspection window | US federal rule for pre-1978 homes, often 10 days, and often waived in contracts | Waived by a rider the buyer did not read |
| Rate lock expiry | Lender | Relock at a worse rate or pay an extension fee |
| Final walkthrough | Usually within 24 to 48 hours of closing | Broken systems become the buyer's problem |

## 4. Things that are easy to misunderstand

- **A verbal accepted offer may bind nobody.** In attorney-driven markets either side can walk until the contract is signed and the deposit is paid. Money spent on inspections before that is at risk (budget a few thousand dollars that may be lost).
- **After signing, the exits are few.** Where the contract has no inspection contingency, the loan contingency may be the only one left, and a low appraisal may not trigger it if the loan amount is fixed low.
- **Oral promises can vanish at closing.** Contracts often say that statements not written into the contract, or not written to survive closing, end at closing (called merger). Anything the seller promised must be written in, item by item.
- **Down payment percentage in the offer is a signal.** It tells the seller the loan is safe. Changing it later may be possible before the contract is signed; after signing it is a visible renegotiation.
- **During underwriting, do not change the file.** No new credit cards, car loans, job changes, or large unexplained transfers. Ask the lender how gift funds must be documented before moving them.

## 5. Rough timeline

From accepted offer to closing, 8 to 14 weeks is common for financed purchases. Title problems (estates, life estates, liens) and slow specialty inspections are the usual causes of delay. The only part fully under the buyer's control is the period before contract signing, so schedule tests early.
